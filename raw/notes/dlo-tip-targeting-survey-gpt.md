## Direct matches

**Iterative Residual Policy for Goal-Conditioned Dynamic Manipulation of Deformable Objects** is the strongest direct hit. Task setup: a UR5, extended with a 50 cm wooden stick, whips a rope so the tip hits a target location in the air; in the verified setup, the goal is defined in the robot’s **Y–Z plane**, and success is measured by the minimum distance from the tip trajectory to the goal. Sim vs. real: trained in **MuJoCo** on a large simulation dataset and evaluated both in simulation and on multiple real ropes, including out-of-distribution cases such as a bullwhip and cloth-like ropes. Learning method: an **iterative residual policy** that learns a **delta-dynamics network** on rasterized tip-trajectory images, samples candidate delta actions online, and greedily executes the one predicted to reduce goal distance the most. Action representation: a **3-parameter whipping primitive** \(a=(v, J_2, J_3)\), where \(v\) is max angular velocity and \(J_2, J_3\) are joint targets; the policy updates this action via sampled delta actions. This is the clearest published example of “learn a policy that swings/whips a rope tip to a specified target point,” with the caveat that the verified target space is planar rather than unconstrained 3D. citeturn14view0turn12view1turn13view2turn13view3

**Self-Supervised Learning of Dynamic Planar Manipulation of Free-End Cables** is the most relevant adjacent paper if your real interest is “dynamic goal-conditioned cable endpoint reaching,” even though the target lies on a plane rather than in the air. Task setup: a UR5 continuously grips one end of a free cable and executes a fast planar motion so the cable endpoint reaches a designated planar target, possibly beyond the arm’s workspace. Sim vs. real: the pipeline combines a tuned **PyBullet** simulator with real UR5 experiments on three cables. Learning method: a **learned forward dynamics model** predicts the endpoint reached by each candidate action; the controller samples many candidate actions and chooses the one whose predicted endpoint is closest to the goal. Action representation: a low-dimensional **two-arc planar motion primitive** with fixed system parameters and four varying action variables, consisting of three polar coefficients plus an added wrist rotation parameter. For anyone building rope-tip target reaching, this is one of the most reusable papers after IRP. citeturn27view0turn28view0

**Real2Sim2Real: Self-Supervised Learning of Physical Single-Step Dynamic Actions for Planar Robot Casting** is an earlier free-end cable target-reaching paper from the same Berkeley line. Task setup: a robot arm holds one end of a cable and executes a single planar motion so the free end slides across a surface toward a desired target. Sim vs. real: a small set of physical trajectories is used to tune multiple simulators, and the final policy is trained on a weighted combination of physical and large simulated datasets. Learning method: **self-supervised simulator tuning with Differential Evolution**, followed by supervised policy learning from mixed sim/real data. Action representation: a **parameterized two-sweep arc primitive** with a wrist rotation term and a max-velocity term, converted to smooth robot motion by spline generation and jerk-limited control. This is planar rather than aerial striking, but it is one of the cleanest precedents for **goal-conditioned one-shot dynamic DLO control**. citeturn10view0turn11view0

**Robots of the Lost Arc: Self-Supervised Learning to Dynamically Manipulate Fixed-Endpoint Cables** is not a tip-to-point paper, but it is a direct methodological precursor for dynamic cable policies. Task setup: a UR5 manipulates a cable fixed at one end to **vault over obstacles**, **knock a target object from a pedestal**, and **weave** between obstacles. Sim vs. real: a simulator is used to find a viable bootstrap action, then the UR5 collects self-supervised real data across five physical cables. Learning method: **self-supervised deep imitation learning** with behavior cloning from successful trials. Action representation: the policy predicts a **3D apex point** for a **minimum-jerk ballistic trajectory**; the start and end arm configurations are fixed by task, while the midpoint/apex changes the cable motion. This paper matters because it shows that a very low-dimensional dynamic primitive can be enough to learn target-conditioned cable behaviors on real hardware. citeturn9view0turn8view0turn8view2turn9view1

## Dynamic DLO papers adjacent to the exact task

**Wiggle and Go! System Identification for Zero-Shot Dynamic Rope Manipulation** is the closest recent paper to the full “3D target-point striking” formulation, but it is **not** a learned runtime policy in the same sense as IRP. Task setup: an xArm 7 with a pole extender performs **3D point striking**, **lobbing**, and **draping** with ropes; the 3D point-striking task minimizes the rope-tip’s 3D Euclidean distance to the target. Sim vs. real: the method trains a system-identification network in simulation, performs a single “wiggle” observation on the real rope, predicts rope parameters, and then optimizes a task trajectory for real execution. Learning method: a **task-agnostic system-identification network** predicts rope parameters from a safe observation action; task execution then uses **CMA-ES trajectory optimization** in **Drake**. Action representation: **three sequential joint-space waypoints** interpolated by cubic splines; this is 21D for the full 7-DOF arm or 9D in planar-restricted variants. If your true requirement is “3D point striking,” this is the most relevant 2026 paper, but it solves the last-mile control by optimization, not by a learned policy head. citeturn29view3turn30view0turn29view0turn29view2

**DexDLO: Learning Goal-Conditioned Dexterous Policy for Dynamic Manipulation of Deformable Linear Objects** is important because it reframes several DLO tasks as **goal-conditioned** dexterous manipulation problems, including **DLO end-tip position control**. Task setup: a fixed-base dexterous hand manipulates deformable linear objects in MuJoCo across tasks such as grabbing, pulling, and end-tip position control. Sim vs. real: the verified source I found describes **MuJoCo simulation** results rather than real-robot results. Learning method: a **model-free goal-conditioned policy-learning framework** for dynamic DLO manipulation. Action representation: the accessible abstract/source snippet clearly establishes low-level dexterous hand control in simulation, but the exact command type was not explicit in the source I could verify, so I would treat the action space as “low-level hand control” rather than claim torque versus position targets. This is highly relevant conceptually, but less directly useful than IRP if your manipulator is a standard arm with one gripper. citeturn4search7turn4search11

**Learning Deformable Object Manipulation Using Task-Level Iterative Learning Control** is not a goal-point paper, but it is a strong recent example of data-efficient learning for dynamic rope manipulation on hardware. Task setup: a non-planar **flying knot** task learned from a single human demonstration across seven rope types. Sim vs. real: the method learns **directly on hardware** while using a simplified rope model. Learning method: **Task-Level Iterative Learning Control**, where a local inverse model is computed by solving a quadratic program that maps task-space errors into command updates. Action representation: a **time-dependent feedforward command trajectory**, not a state-feedback policy; the algorithm iteratively corrects the command sequence trial by trial. This paper is especially relevant if you care more about **rapid real-world adaptation** than about a fully amortized policy. citeturn40search0turn40search1

**Dynamic Manipulation of Deformable Objects With Implicit Integration** is the main non-learning benchmark to know about for “whip to hit a target.” Task setup: a dual-arm YuMi uses a soft rod as a **whip** to hit target objects and also solves other dynamic soft-body tasks defined by target positions of selected points over time. Sim vs. real: the paper includes both simulation and real-world executions. Learning method: **none**; it is a model-based optimal control paper comparing **batch Newton** and **DDP** under a soft-body simulator with **implicit integration**. Action representation: an **open-loop joint-space control sequence** optimized against a differentiable deformable-body model. For your problem, this is the strongest “classical optimizer baseline” to compare against learned methods. citeturn32view0

## Rigid-object analogs

**TossingBot: Learning to Throw Arbitrary Objects with Residual Physics** is the canonical rigid-object analog. Task setup: a robot grasps arbitrary rigid objects from a bin and throws them into selected boxes outside its kinematic reach. Sim vs. real: real robot system with learned throwing performance, informed by analytical physics and data-driven residuals. Learning method: an end-to-end formulation that learns **control parameters for grasping and throwing motion primitives** from visual observations through trial and error, while adding **learned residual corrections** on top of a physics simulator’s predictions. Action representation: parameterized **grasp** and **throw motion primitives** with learned control parameters. Conceptually, this is the cleanest analog for “estimate a low-dimensional dynamic action conditioned on a target, then learn the physics residual the simulator gets wrong.” citeturn44view0turn44view1

**Dynamic Handover: Throw and Catch with Bimanual Hands** is a good analog for learned dynamic object transfer rather than target-hitting. Task setup: one dexterous hand throws and another catches diverse objects at high speed. Sim vs. real: trained in simulation and transferred to real robots. Learning method: **multi-agent reinforcement learning** plus a learned **object trajectory prediction model** that helps the catcher estimate where the object is heading. Action representation: the verified abstract establishes learned arm/hand control policies, but the exact action parameterization was not explicit in the source I could verify. I include it because it is one of the better recent examples of **dynamic, high-speed, goal-sensitive manipulation with learned perception and sim-to-real transfer**. citeturn44view2turn44view3

**Learning Accurate Whole-body Throwing with High-frequency Residual Policy and Pullback Tube Acceleration** is a useful 2025 analog if you want a hybrid “policy + model-based correction” design. Task setup: a legged mobile manipulator throws grasped objects to specified targets identified by AprilTags. Sim vs. real: extensive simulation and hardware validation. Learning method: a **nominal RL throwing policy**, a **high-frequency residual policy**, and a model-based **pullback tube acceleration** optimizer for robustness to release uncertainty. Action representation: the policy outputs **PD targets for each of the robot’s 18 joints**, with residual corrections and optimizer-generated end-effector accelerations layered on top. The important parallel to rope whipping is architectural: a learned nominal controller can be materially improved by a fast residual correction layer and a small amount of physics-aware optimization. citeturn42view0

**Learning the Skill of Archery by a Humanoid Robot iCub** is old, but still a very crisp target-conditioned dynamic-manipulation analog. Task setup: a 53-DOF humanoid learns to shoot arrows so they hit the center of a target, using visual feedback from a camera. Sim vs. real: the paper includes both simulation comparisons and real-robot results. Learning method: the authors compare **PoWER** and their own **ARCHER** regression-based method. Action representation: a **3D vector describing the relative position of the robot’s two hands**, which controls shot direction and velocity while the rest of the motion is handled by inverse kinematics. This is worth reading because it demonstrates, very explicitly, that hard ballistic tasks can often be learned through an **extremely low-dimensional action embedding** rather than full trajectory learning. citeturn43view2

## Classical whip and casting foundations

The foundational robotics line here is the **Yamakawa–Namiki–Ishikawa** series: **Motion Planning for Dynamic Knotting of a Flexible Rope With a High-Speed Robot Arm** (IROS 2010), **Simple Model and Deformation Control of a Flexible Rope Using Constant, High-speed Motion of a Robot Arm** (ICRA 2012), and **Dynamic High-Speed Knotting of a Rope by a Manipulator** (2013). Across later summaries of this line, these papers are described as using **high-speed robot-arm motion** to whip ropes and tie knots, relying on a **simplified algebraic or time-delay-style deformation model** under assumptions where gravity can often be neglected. They are not learned-policy papers, but they are the cleanest robotics foundation for the idea that **rope deformation can sometimes be controlled through carefully structured high-speed arm motions plus a very compact object model**. citeturn7view4turn6view1turn27view0

A second useful classical thread is **Casting manipulation of unknown string by robot arm** and its 3D extension **Motion Planning for Dynamic Three-Dimensional Manipulation for Unknown Flexible Linear Object**. The 2021 IROS paper formulates dynamic string casting with a normal robot arm instead of a custom casting manipulator and alternates among **motion generation**, **actual string manipulation**, and **string parameter estimation** until the simulator matches reality closely enough to cast the string toward the target. The 2024 extension explicitly states that it extends the same approach into **3D** and targets desired objects with the flexible linear object’s tip. These are not learned policies, but they are very relevant if you want a system-identification-heavy baseline for unknown ropes. citeturn21view0turn19view0

A third foundation is **Using a compliant, unactuated tail to manipulate objects**. Later cable papers describe it as a system that uses a rope-like appendage to **strike objects of interest at high speed**, with planning based on **RRT** and a **particle-based uncertainty representation**. It is not about ropes held by a conventional arm gripper, but it is one of the cleanest task formulations of “flexible appendage used as a dynamic striker.” citeturn27view0turn11view0

## Simulation and modeling stacks

If you want a **practical simulator stack** for learning, the recent rope-learning papers themselves already span most of the design space. The Berkeley free-end cable paper uses **PyBullet** with the cable modeled as a string of rigid capsules connected by **6-DOF spring constraints**, then tunes friction, damping, stiffness, and endpoint mass parameters with **Differential Evolution**. IRP uses **MuJoCo** with a rope represented by **25 linked capsules** and varies rope length and density across a broad parameter grid. WaG uses **Drake** with a **ball-joint rope chain** and explicitly estimates nine rope parameters before trajectory optimization. Zimmermann’s work uses a much heavier **FEM + implicit integration** stack with **BDF2**, suitable when you need stable soft-body trajectory optimization rather than fast policy-data generation. citeturn28view0turn14view0turn30view0turn32view0

For **foundational rod models**, **Discrete Elastic Rods** remains one of the key references. It proposes a discrete geometric model of thin flexible rods with bending and twisting and has become a core conceptual basis for later DLO simulators. A closely related continuum-mechanics direction is **CORDE: Cosserat Rod Elements for the Dynamic Simulation of One-Dimensional Elastic Objects**, which builds a torsion-aware dynamic simulation model for ropes and similar one-dimensional elastic objects from **Cosserat rod theory**. These are still highly relevant because much of the modern DLO-learning literature either approximates them with capsule chains or differentiates through close relatives of them. citeturn34search0turn34search1turn34search4

For newer **learning-compatible simulators/models**, the strongest paper I found is **DEFORM: Differentiable Discrete Elastic Rods for Real-Time Modeling of Deformable Linear Objects**, published at CoRL 2024. Its focus is long-horizon dynamic DLO modeling, but the paper explicitly combines a **differentiable DER-style physics model** with learning, validates on two industrial robots, and demonstrates autonomous planning and control of DLOs. If you want a modern training backbone for rope or cable manipulation that is more physically grounded than a pure capsule-chain baseline, this is probably the first simulator/model paper to try. citeturn38view0

For an **off-the-shelf Cosserat-rod software option**, **PyElastica** is the main open-source reference I would keep in view. Its documentation and repository describe it as an open-source simulator for assemblies of slender one-dimensional bodies using **Cosserat rod theory**, including external and internal forces and self-contact. It is not a rope-whipping paper by itself, but it is one of the most straightforward ways to stand up a continuous rod simulation environment if you want to move beyond capsule chains. citeturn37search1turn37search6turn37search0

## Open questions and limitations

The most important gap is still the one you pointed to: a **major-venue, real-robot, fully goal-conditioned learned policy** that makes a rope tip hit an **arbitrary 3D target point in free space**. The literature I found either keeps the task **planar** while learning a runtime policy, or moves to **3D** by doing **system identification plus trajectory optimization** instead of amortized policy learning. citeturn17view12turn27view0turn29view3

A second gap is that most successful systems still rely on **very low-dimensional action parameterizations** such as apex points, two-arc sweeps, or a few joint-space waypoints. That is probably a feature rather than a bug, but it means there is still relatively little evidence that high-capacity end-to-end policies outperform carefully designed motion primitives in this domain. citeturn9view0turn11view0turn28view0turn30view0turn43view2
