## Definition

A low-dimensional action representation for dynamic manipulation in which a complete arm trajectory is reduced to a single waypoint — the **apex point**, a 3D position or 3-joint configuration at the trajectory's midpoint — and the rest of the motion is generated by a fixed planner that interpolates start, apex, and end under kinematic and dynamic feasibility constraints. The learned policy regresses only the apex; the planner fills in timing, velocity, acceleration, and jerk profiles.

In the canonical instantiation (INDy, [[robots-lost-arc-self-supervised-learning]]), the planner is a quadratic program that minimizes squared jerk subject to joint, velocity, acceleration, and jerk limits, and the start/apex/end equality constraints; the QP horizon is repeatedly shrunk until infeasible to make the motion as fast as the robot allows.

## Intuition

Dynamic manipulation policies usually have to choose between two extremes: regress a high-dimensional trajectory (hard to learn, easy to violate dynamics) or regress a parametric primitive like a fixed arc (easy to learn, brittle across tasks). The apex-point parameterization splits the difference: the planner is the inductive bias for *how* a trajectory should look (smooth, fast, feasible), while the network only has to decide *where* the trajectory should peak. Because a single 3D point is enough to disambiguate most ballistic motions for cable-like deformables, the network sees an easy regression target and the data demand collapses to ~hundreds of real-world trials.

## Formal notation

Let $\pi : \mathcal{O} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$ be the learned policy mapping observations to an apex configuration $a$, and $f_{\mathcal{A}_i} : \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ a task-specific trajectory function fixing the start and end configurations:

$$
\tau = f_{\mathcal{A}_i}(a), \quad a = \pi(o).
$$

The trajectory function is realized as a min-jerk QP

$$
\min_{(\vec q,\vec v,\vec a)_{0:H}} \sum_{t=0}^{H-1} (\vec a_{t+1}-\vec a_t)^\top Q (\vec a_{t+1}-\vec a_t)
$$

s.t. $\vec q_0$, $\vec q_{\lfloor H/2 \rfloor}=a$, $\vec q_H$ fixed; $\vec v_0=\vec v_H=0$; Newton-step dynamics $\vec q_{t+1}=\vec q_t+h\vec v_t+\tfrac12 h^2 \vec a_t$, $\vec v_{t+1}=\vec v_t+h\vec a_t$; box constraints on $\vec q$, $\vec v$, $\vec a$, and $\vec a_{t+1}-\vec a_t$. Decrement $H$ until infeasible to obtain the fastest feasible motion.

## Variants

- **Joint-space apex** (INDy): the 3D point lives in (base, shoulder, elbow) joint angles; the remaining joints are tied linearly to the first three.
- **Cartesian apex**: the 3D point lives in end-effector $(x,y,z)$ and the planner solves IK at the apex.
- **2D throw apex** (TossingBot, [[tossingbot-learning-throw-arbitrary-objects-residual]]): a closely-related 2-parameter regression target (residual velocity over a physics prior) for free-flight projectiles. INDy is the 3D arm-trajectory analog of this idea.
- **Multi-waypoint extensions**: future work envisions stacking apex points (two or more midpoints) when a single arc is no longer expressive enough — for longer cables, free-end manipulation, or whip-style multi-phase motions.

## Comparison

- vs **full-trajectory regression**: lower data demand, dynamically feasible by construction, but cannot express motions whose shape is not well-summarized by a single midpoint.
- vs **dynamic movement primitives (DMPs)**: DMPs parameterize an entire shape with shape parameters; apex-point keeps a single waypoint and offloads the shape to a fixed solver.
- vs **iterative residual policies** ([[iterative-residual-policy-goal-conditioned-dynamic]]): residual policies adapt actions across episodes after observing outcomes; apex-point is open-loop within an episode but can be combined with a residual outer loop.

## When to use

- Deformable-object dynamic manipulation where the target distribution is well-summarized by a single midpoint (e.g., a single arc clears the obstacle / strikes the target).
- Real-robot self-supervised loops where data is expensive and the policy must converge from $\sim$100s of trials.
- Pipelines that want to keep the dynamics opaque (no analytical model of the cable) but still respect joint and jerk limits.

## Known limitations

- Single-waypoint expressivity ceiling: longer cables, multi-phase motions, and free-end whip dynamics break the assumption that one apex is enough — INDy itself collapses at 6.2m cable length.
- Open-loop within an episode: no closed-loop correction once the arm leaves the start pose.
- Reset dependence: the parameterization assumes a reproducible initial state (e.g., taut-pull reset of a fixed-endpoint cable). Without a reset trick the trajectory becomes a function of the variable initial state, defeating the dimensionality reduction.

## Open problems

- A principled rule for when to add a second apex (or a higher-dimensional waypoint set) to escape the single-arc ceiling.
- Learning the start and end configurations jointly with the apex, instead of fixing them per task.
- Conditioning the apex on cable physical parameters (length, mass per length) for cross-cable generalization.

## Key papers

- [[robots-lost-arc-self-supervised-learning]] — introduces the 3D-apex + min-jerk-QP formulation for fixed-endpoint cable vaulting/knocking/weaving.

## My understanding

The apex-point parameterization is best read as TossingBot's 2D throw vector lifted into 3D arm space, with a min-jerk QP standing in for free-flight ballistics. It works because deformable cables are hard to model dynamically, but their *outcome distributions* are smooth functions of a single midpoint when the start pose is reproducible. The natural follow-up is to break the single-waypoint ceiling either by composing apexes (two-apex weaving), by closing the loop (residual or iterative apex updates per attempt), or by conditioning on physical parameters so one apex policy generalizes across cable lengths. All three are open.
